# A Plane Ran Out of Fuel at 41,000 Feet — But Everyone Survived

**Format:** Bright Side–style narrated edutainment
**Target length:** ~9 minutes (~1,450 words VO)
**Visuals:** 2D/3D animation + stock aviation footage + motion-graphic infographics
**Thumbnail:** Boeing 767 gliding with both engines dark, red arrow to an empty fuel gauge. Caption: "0 FUEL. 41,000 FT."
**True story:** Air Canada Flight 143, July 23, 1983 — the "Gimli Glider"

---

## HOOK (0:00 – 0:35)

**[VISUAL: Interior cabin POV, seat-back view, warm evening light through windows. Sudden silence — engine hum cuts out.]**

**NARRATOR:** You're 41,000 feet over Canada, cruising in a brand-new Boeing 767. You've just finished your snack, you lean back, close your eyes… and that's when you notice it. The sound. Or actually — the *lack* of it.

Both engines have just gone completely silent.

**[VISUAL: Exterior shot — engines spooling down, fan blades slowing.]**

Your 132-ton airliner has officially become the world's heaviest glider. The screens in the cockpit go dark. And the nearest airport is too far away to reach.

**[VISUAL: Freeze-frame, record-scratch. Zoom to a small airfield with go-karts racing on the runway.]**

But here's the wild part: what the captain does next has never been attempted in a commercial jet — and it will save every single life on board. Oh, and the only "runway" he can reach? At this exact moment, it's hosting a family race day. With crowds. And go-karts. *On the runway.*

Buckle up — this is the true story of the Gimli Glider.

## SEGMENT 1 — THE INVISIBLE MISTAKE (0:35 – 2:10)

**[VISUAL: Montreal airport, 1983; retro color grade; ground crew with clipboards.]**

**NARRATOR:** July 23, 1983. Air Canada Flight 143 is getting ready to fly from Montreal to Edmonton, with a quick stop in Ottawa. The aircraft is the pride of the fleet — a Boeing 767, so new it still has that new-plane smell. It's one of the first airliners with a fully computerized "glass cockpit."

There's just one problem. The fuel gauges aren't working.

**[VISUAL: Close-up of blank fuel quantity indicators; a yellow "INOP" sticker slapped on.]**

A faulty fuel-quantity computer means the crew can't see how much fuel is on board. No big deal, everyone thinks — we'll just measure the tanks manually with a dripstick, do a little math, and calculate the fuel by weight. What could go wrong?

**[VISUAL: Chalkboard-style animation: "22,300" appears with a question mark.]**

Well. In 1983, Canada was right in the middle of switching from the imperial system… to metric. The 767 was the first Air Canada plane calculated in *kilograms*. But everyone on the ground that day was used to *pounds*.

The flight needs about 22,300 **kilograms** of fuel. The crew multiplies using the conversion factor for **pounds**.

**[VISUAL: Fuel truck animation; tank fills to less than half; a happy checkmark appears — then glitches.]**

The math looks perfect. The paperwork looks perfect. But Flight 143 takes off with roughly **half** the fuel it needs.

And nobody on board has any idea.

---

## SEGMENT 2 — 41,000 FEET, ZERO FUEL (2:10 – 4:00)

**[VISUAL: Serene cruise over Ontario; passengers reading; sunset tones.]**

**NARRATOR:** For the first hour, everything is textbook. Sixty-one passengers, eight crew, smooth skies. Captain Bob Pearson and First Officer Maurice Quintal are relaxed.

Then, over Red Lake, Ontario — *ding.*

**[VISUAL: Cockpit warning chime; amber light: LOW FUEL PRESSURE, left tank.]**

A warning nobody's ever heard in a 767 before. Low fuel pressure in the left tank. Must be a failed fuel pump, the pilots think — after all, the paperwork says they have plenty of fuel. They decide to divert to Winnipeg, just to be safe.

*Ding.* Now it's the right tank.

**[VISUAL: Warning lights multiplying across the panel.]**

Minutes later, the left engine flames out. And then the crew hears a long, loud *BONG* — a sound no 767 crew had ever heard outside a simulator. Both engines: gone. Total power loss at 41,000 feet.

**[VISUAL: Cockpit screens flickering off one by one, leaving a few tiny backup dials.]**

And here's something most people don't know: on this plane, the engines power almost *everything*. No engines means the fancy glass cockpit goes dark, leaving just a few basic backup instruments. It also means no normal hydraulics — the muscles that move the wings' control surfaces.

**[VISUAL: Cutaway animation of a small propeller dropping from the belly of the plane.]**

Quick side note — this is genuinely cool. For exactly this nightmare, the 767 hides a secret gadget: the **ram air turbine**, a little propeller that pops out of the belly and uses the wind itself to generate emergency hydraulic power. Basically, a tiny windmill is now helping fly a 132-ton jet. Nature: 1, fancy computers: 0.

But a windmill can't restart the engines. The plane is falling about 20 feet for every second that passes. Winnipeg is 65 miles away.

They're not going to make it.

## SEGMENT 3 — ONE PILOT'S SECRET WEAPON (4:00 – 5:40)

**[VISUAL: Split screen — stressed cockpit / calm glider soaring over green hills.]**

**NARRATOR:** Now, if you're going to lose both engines in a jetliner, here's the one pilot you want up front. Because Captain Bob Pearson has an unusual hobby.

He flies **gliders** on the weekends.

**[VISUAL: Pearson's hands on the controls; overlay of glide-ratio diagram: 12:1.]**

While the cabin crew quietly prepares the passengers, Pearson does something airline pilots are never trained to do — he starts flying the 767 like a giant glider, feeling for its best gliding speed. Meanwhile, First Officer Quintal grabs what's basically a paper chart and starts doing life-or-death math by hand: altitude lost versus distance covered. The verdict is brutal. Winnipeg is out of reach. Completely.

Then Quintal remembers something. Years ago, in the Air Force, he was stationed at a base nearby: **Gimli**. It has a nice, long runway. It's close enough. It's their only option.

**[VISUAL: Map zooms to Gimli; then the runway splits — one half becomes a drag strip with crowds, campers, barbecues.]**

What Quintal doesn't know? Gimli isn't an Air Force base anymore. Half of it is now a **racetrack**. And on this beautiful summer Saturday, it's packed — race cars, families, kids on bikes, campers parked right beside the strip.

Sixty-one passengers above. Hundreds of people below. And a silent, 132-ton glider is coming down, whether anyone likes it or not.

---

## SEGMENT 4 — THE IMPOSSIBLE LANDING (5:40 – 7:20)

**[VISUAL: Final approach; altitude counter spinning down; total silence except wind.]**

**NARRATOR:** As they line up with Gimli, Pearson realizes they have a new problem — and it's a big one. They're too **high**. Sounds like a good problem, right? It's not. Too high means overshooting the runway and plowing into the fields — or the crowd. And in a plane with no engines, you can't circle around for a second try. You get exactly one shot.

A normal jet would deploy flaps and spoilers. No hydraulic power means: not today.

So Pearson reaches into his weekend-hobby toolbox and pulls out a move called a **sideslip**.

**[VISUAL: Slow-mo animation — the jet banks sideways, one wing dropped, flying almost diagonally, bleeding altitude fast.]**

He crosses the controls and tilts the massive airliner *sideways* — using the plane's own body as a giant airbrake. Glider pilots do this all the time. In a commercial jetliner, with passengers watching the ground fill their windows? Essentially unheard of. Passengers later said they could see the faces of people on the ground.

At the last moment, Pearson straightens out. The main wheels slam down at about 175 knots. And instantly — problem number… honestly, we've lost count. Without full power, the nose gear never locked in place.

**[VISUAL: Nose gear collapsing; nose grinding down the strip; sparks; guardrail contact; people below scattering.]**

The nose collapses. The plane grinds down the drag strip on its face in a shower of sparks — which, believe it or not, is *helping*, acting like a brake. Ahead, race-day crowds scatter. The giant glider shrieks, slows… and stops. Right there on the strip. Short of everyone.

**[VISUAL: Beat of silence. Then evacuation slides pop; passengers slide down; wide shot of the intact plane.]**

Every single person on board — all 69 — walks away. The only injuries? A few bumps and scrapes… from going down the evacuation slides. The people on the ground? Untouched.

## SEGMENT 5 — THE AFTERMATH (7:20 – 8:30)

**[VISUAL: Newspaper headlines spinning: "THE GIMLI GLIDER."]**

**NARRATOR:** The world gives the plane a legendary nickname: **the Gimli Glider**. Pearson and Quintal are eventually celebrated for one of the greatest feats of airmanship in history, and their story changes how crews around the world handle fuel checks — so a mix-up like this can basically never happen again. One tiny conversion error, caught forever.

And the plane itself? Here's the part nobody believes.

**[VISUAL: The same aircraft, repaired, in service; timeline 1983 → 2008.]**

It was repaired… and flew passengers for **25 more years**.

But the absolute best detail of this whole story? When Air Canada sent a team of mechanics *by road* to go fix the plane at Gimli… their van **ran out of gas** on the way. You just can't make this stuff up.

**[VISUAL: Cartoon van sputtering on a highway shoulder; facepalm emoji.]**
